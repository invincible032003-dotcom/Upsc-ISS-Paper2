#### 3.1d General properties / mixed statements

- **[2020·Q5]** What must a good estimator be — efficient, consistent, unbiased, sufficient?

**Results ledger.**
- $\text{MSE}(T)=V(T)+[\text{Bias}(T)]^2$. Unbiased $\Rightarrow$ MSE $=$ variance. **No estimator minimises MSE uniformly over $\Theta$** (take $T\equiv\theta_0$ as a competitor at $\theta_0$).
- **Sufficient condition for consistency:** $E(T_n)\to\theta$ and $V(T_n)\to0$ (via Chebyshev). Unbiasedness alone does not give consistency; consistency does not give unbiasedness.
- Consistency is preserved under continuous transformation (Slutsky): $T\xrightarrow{P}\theta\Rightarrow g(T)\xrightarrow{P}g(\theta)$; sums and products of consistent estimators are consistent. Unbiasedness is **not** preserved: $T$ unbiased for $\theta$ does not make $T^2$ unbiased for $\theta^2$.
- Unbiased estimators are generally **not unique** (any convex combination of two is unbiased $\Rightarrow$ infinitely many); they may fail to exist (e.g. $1/p$ in Bernoulli).
- **Efficiency–correlation identity.** If $T_0$ is MVU and $T_1$ has efficiency $e$, then $\rho(T_0,T_1)=\sqrt e$. Consequences: $\operatorname{Cov}(T_0,T_1)=V(T_0)$, $\operatorname{Cov}(T_0,T_1-T_0)=0$. **This one identity answers 2018·Q20, 2019·Q50, 2020·Q25, 2022·Q33, 2023·Q7(1), 2024·Q3.**
- For two unbiased estimators with equal variance and common efficiency $e$: $\rho\ge 2e-1$.
- Any unbiased linear combination $aT_0+(1-a)T_1$ with $a\ne1$ has variance strictly exceeding $V(T_0)$ — so it is **never** MVUE. (2023·Q7(2), 2026·Q16, 2026·Q23.)
- Efficiency of sample median relative to sample mean for normal data $=2/\pi\approx0.637$.
- For $U(0,\theta)$: $V(2\bar X)=\theta^2/(3n)$ and $V\!\left(\frac{n+1}nX_{(n)}\right)=\frac{\theta^2}{n(n+2)}$, so the order statistic is far superior — relative efficiency $\frac{3}{n+2}$.
- For a symmetric unimodal distribution mean $=$ median $=$ mode; for the normal the sample mean is the efficient estimator of all three.

**Results ledger.**
- **Fisher–Neyman factorization:** $T$ is sufficient $\iff f(x;\theta)=g_\theta(T(x))\cdot h(x)$ with $h$ free of $\theta$.
- Equivalent definition: conditional distribution of the sample given $T$ is free of $\theta$.
- Standard sufficient statistics:

 | Family | Sufficient statistic |
 |---|---|
 | Bernoulli/Binomial($n,p$) | $\sum X_i$ |
 | Poisson($\lambda$) | $\sum X_i$ |
 | $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, both unknown | $(\sum X_i,\sum X_i^2)$ |
 | $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$, $\mu$ known | $\sum(X_i-\mu)^2$ |
 | Exponential mean $\theta$ | $\sum X_i$ |
 | $\theta x^{\theta-1}$ on $(0,1)$ | $\prod X_i$ (equivalently $\sum\log X_i$) |
 | $U(0,\theta)$ | $X_{(n)}$ |
 | $U(\alpha,\beta)$ or $U(\theta-\tfrac12,\theta+\tfrac12)$ | $(X_{(1)},X_{(n)})$ |
 | Shifted exponential $e^{-(x-\theta)}$, $x>\theta$ | $X_{(1)}$ |
 | $f=\theta/x^2$, $x\ge\theta$ | $X_{(1)}$ |
 | Discrete uniform $\{1..N\}$ | $X_{(n)}$ |
 | Gamma($\alpha,\beta$) both unknown | $(\sum X_i,\prod X_i)$ |
 | Rayleigh $\frac x\theta e^{-x^2/2\theta}$ | $\sum X_i^2$ |
 | $N(\theta,\theta^2)$ | $(\sum X_i,\sum X_i^2)$ |

- Any **one-to-one function** of a sufficient statistic is sufficient (hence $\bar X$ and $\sum X_i$ are equally sufficient); a **many-to-one** reduction generally is not.
- Partitioning the sample and reporting group totals of an additive-sufficiency family (Poisson, Bernoulli) is still sufficient — **the point of 2023·Q41–42**: any vector whose components sum to the sufficient total retains sufficiency, but with **unequal weights** ($X_1+2X_2$) it fails.
- The Cauchy and double-exponential families admit **no** non-trivial sufficient statistic — the full order statistic is minimal sufficient.

**Results ledger.**
- $\lambda=\dfrac{\sup_{\Theta_0}L}{\sup_\Theta L}\in[0,1]$; reject for small $\lambda$.
- **Wilks:** $-2\log\lambda\xrightarrow{d}\chi^2_r$ where $r$ is the difference in the number of free parameters. "Asymptotically normal" is a standard distractor (2024·Q29 IV).
- The LRT is a **function of every sufficient statistic**; for simple vs simple it coincides with the NP test.
- For $N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ both unknown, the LRT for $\mu$ reduces to the two-sided $t$-test, which is the **UMPU** test (2018·Q43); the correct answer is UMPU, not UMP — no UMP test exists two-sided.
- For $N(\mu_i,1)$ with $H_0:\mu_i=0$ for all $i$, the LRT is based on $\sum X_i^2$.
- The LRT is **not necessarily UMP** even when a UMP test exists, but it is consistent under regularity conditions.
- NP applies to **simple vs simple** only — 2026·Q49(I) is deliberately mis-stated.

### SI-16 · Randomized tests
*11 items · S2.19*

- **[2018·Q39]** Poisson, $n=1$, $\omega=\{x>3\}$, $H_0:\lambda=1$ vs $H_1:\lambda=2$: Type-I error probability.
- **[2019·Q62]** $\phi(x)=1/\gamma/0$ according as $f_1\gtrless kf_0$: with $\gamma>0$, is $\phi$ randomised?
- **[2020·Q3]** pmf table; $\varphi=1$ for $X\in\{1,2\}$, $0.25$ for $X=3$: is it MP at level 0.03? size and power?
- **[2022·Q39]** pmf table; reject if $X=0$, and if $X=1$ reject when two coins both show heads: Type-I error.
- **[2022·Q40]** Same: Type-II error.
- **[2023·Q37]** Poisson $n=4$; $\varphi=1$ if $\sum x_i>2$, $0.1$ if $=2$, else 0; $H_0:\lambda=1$ vs $H_1:\lambda=1.5$: size interval.
- **[2023·Q38]** Same: power interval.
- **[2023·Q39]** pmf table; reject if $X=0$, and if $X=4$ reject on a head: size.
- **[2023·Q40]** Same: power.
- **[2024·Q43]** Poisson, $n=1$; reject if $x>1$, and if $x=1$ reject on a head: Type-I error.
- **[2024·Q44]** Same: Type-II error.

**Results ledger.**
- A randomised test is a function $\varphi:\mathcal X\to[0,1]$; $\varphi(x)$ is the probability of rejecting when $x$ is observed. It is non-randomised iff $\varphi$ takes only the values 0 and 1.
- Size $=E_{H_0}[\varphi(X)]=\sum_x\varphi(x)P_0(x)$; power $=E_{H_1}[\varphi(X)]$.
- The coin-toss constructions are just $\varphi=\tfrac12$ (one coin, head) or $\varphi=\tfrac14$ (two coins, both heads) at the designated outcome.
- Randomisation exists to hit an **exact** size $\alpha$ in discrete problems, where non-randomised tests can only attain a lattice of sizes.
- **This is a purely mechanical cluster** — 11 items, all solvable by the two formulae above. Highest accuracy-per-effort in the testing half of the syllabus.

### SI-19 · Elements of decision theory; loss, risk, Bayes estimators
*10 items · S2.22*

- **[2018·Q48]** Which loss function makes the risk equal to mean squared error?
- **[2018·Q49]** $N(\mu,1)$ with prior $N(0,1)$, squared error loss: the Bayes risk.
- **[2018·Q50]** Bayes estimator under squared error loss.
- **[2018·Q51]** Which is **not** correct: unique Bayes solution admissible / a uniformly best decision rule always exists / posterior median is Bayes under absolute error / constant-risk Bayes rule is minimax.
- **[2019·Q74]** Bayes estimator under squared error loss is the mean of which distribution?
- **[2020·Q10]** Bayes rule under absolute error loss $L=|\theta-d|$.
- **[2020·Q65]** The expectation of the loss function is called?
- **[2021·Q53]** $X\sim B(n,\theta)$, prior Beta($\alpha,\beta$): first posterior parameter $p_1$.
- **[2021·Q54]** Same: second posterior parameter $p_2$.
- **[2024·Q35]** (I) A Bayes estimator with constant risk is minimax; (II) a unique minimax estimator must be admissible.

**Results ledger.**
- Risk $R(\theta,\delta)=E_\theta[L(\theta,\delta(X))]$. With $L=(\theta-a)^2$ the risk is the MSE.
- Bayes estimator under **squared error** loss $=$ posterior **mean**; under **absolute error** loss $=$ posterior **median**; under 0–1 loss $=$ posterior **mode**.
- Beta–binomial conjugacy: prior Beta($\alpha,\beta$), likelihood $B(n,\theta)$ $\Rightarrow$ posterior Beta($x+\alpha,\ n-x+\beta$).
- Normal–normal: $X_i\sim N(\mu,1)$, prior $\mu\sim N(0,1)$ $\Rightarrow$ posterior mean $\frac{\sum x_i}{n+1}$, posterior variance $\frac1{n+1}$, and the Bayes risk is $\frac1{n+1}$.
- A Bayes rule with **constant risk** is minimax. A unique Bayes rule is admissible. A **uniformly best decision rule does not generally exist** — that is the false option in 2018·Q51.
- **Declining trend:** 10 items, but **none since 2024**. Still syllabus-listed; keep the six facts above.

### SI-10 · Bhattacharya bounds
*2 items · S2.7 — named in the syllabus, asked twice, both in 2020*

- **[2020·Q20]** The Bhattacharya bound is a generalisation of which result?
- **[2020·Q31]** $N(\theta,1)$: Bhattacharya bound for $g(\theta)=\theta^2$. *(Options include $\frac{4\theta^2}n+\frac2{n^2}$.)*

**Results ledger.**
- Generalises the Cramér–Rao inequality by using higher-order derivatives of the likelihood: with $J=(J_{ij})$, $J_{ij}=E\!\left[\frac{L^{(i)}}{L}\frac{L^{(j)}}{L}\right]$, the bound is $V(T)\ge\mathbf a'J^{-1}\mathbf a$ where $a_i=\frac{d^i g}{d\theta^i}$.
- The first-order Bhattacharya bound **is** the CR bound; second and higher orders are sharper.
- For $N(\theta,1)$ and $g(\theta)=\theta^2$: the CR bound is $4\theta^2/n$; the second-order Bhattacharya bound adds $2/n^2$, and $\bar X^2-\frac1n$ attains it exactly.
- **Two items in nine years, both in one paper.** Cheap insurance: learn the definition, the "generalises CR" fact, and this one worked example.

### SI-12 · Resampling: bootstrap and jackknife
*2 items · S2.12 — newest addition, both appearances recent*

- **[2023·Q13]** With $n=5$, the number of **distinct** bootstrap samples of size 5 drawn with replacement.
- **[2026·Q39]** How many of bootstrap, jackknife, cross-validation and stratified sampling are **resampling** methods?

**Results ledger.**
- Number of distinct bootstrap resamples (unordered, with replacement) from $n$ observations: $\dbinom{2n-1}{n}$. For $n=5$: $\binom95=126$.
- **Bootstrap:** resample $n$ observations **with** replacement, $B$ times, to approximate the sampling distribution of a statistic.
- **Jackknife:** delete-one (or delete-$d$) systematic resampling; principally a bias- and variance-reduction device. Bias estimate $=(n-1)(\bar\theta_{(\cdot)}-\hat\theta)$.
- **Cross-validation** is a resampling method (for model assessment); **stratified sampling** is a sampling *design*, not a resampling method — the discriminating fact in 2026·Q39.
- **Trend flag:** absent 2018–2022, present in 2023 and 2026. The syllabus names it explicitly; treat as an active area with a very small footprint of examinable content.
